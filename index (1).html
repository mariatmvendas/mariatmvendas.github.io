The City of Dreams

Every year, more than 300,000 people move to New York City in pursuit of a better life. Many drawn by opportunity, others by the promise that ambition alone is enough. The skyline sells the dream. The housing data tells a different story.

Data analysis by [ Maria Vendas & Diogo Forte ] · Final project · DTU

A System at the Breaking Point

As New York's housing stock continues to age, the systems designed to protect its residents are fracturing. Buildings go years without basic repairs. Heating fails. Mold spreads. Structural damage is ignored.

The city's Housing Preservation and Development agency (HPD) tracks this crisis through two lenses: complaints filed directly whenever a tenant calls 311 to report an issue, and formal violations issued after an inspector visits. Together, these datasets reveal who is left behind, and who is responsible.

Part I
The Complaint Crisis
01 The Scale

Volume of Voice: 3 Million Pleas for Repair

Between January 1 of 2021, and December 31 of 2025, HPD received over 3 million complaints. Focusing on the "Big Five" issues: Heat/Hot Water, Unsanitary Conditions, Water Leaks, Plumbing, and Electric, yields a total of 2,468,069 recorded cases.

While the volume of complaints has risen city-wide, the map below reveals a stark geographic reality: housing distress is deeply concentrated. By looking at the city by zip code, we can see exactly where the system is under the most pressure.

03 Income & Distress

Is the Income Gap a Housing Gap?

The data reveals an uncomfortable truth. In New York City, your zip code and your bank account determine whether you live in a safe, functional home. When we overlay median household income against HPD complaint density, the correlation is almost 100%. Areas of concentrated wealth experience almost total housing stability, while the city's lowest-income neighborhoods bear the overwhelming brunt of the housing crisis. Therefore, Bronx and Brooklyn stand out as the boroughs most affected by this disparity. Their low income levels contribute to the persistent housing issues in these areas.

From Complaint to Violation
04 When the System Responds

From Words to Violations: When the City Confirms the Crisis

A complaint is an SOS; a violation is the city's official confirmation that a home is unsafe. Surprisingly, violation counts actually outpace complaints in every borough—often because one inspector's visit reveals a cascade of secondary issues.

Part III
Days to Close Violations
07 Closing the Loop

Both complaints and violations are deeply related to socioeconomic factors. But does the time it takes to resolve a violation also track with income?

For violations that eventually get certified as corrected, the time between inspection and official closure varies dramatically across the city. This chart focuses only on closed violations — meaning cases where a fix was confirmed — and asks a simple question: how many days did that take?

The answer, again, tracks closely with income. In lower-income zip codes, even resolved violations take significantly longer to certify. This could reflect landlord delays, slower HPD re-inspection schedules, or both — but the outcome is the same: tenants in the poorest neighbourhoods wait the longest for their homes to be officially declared safe, even after a fix has supposedly been made.

06 The Waiting Game

Getting a violation on record is one thing. Getting it fixed is another.

Once a violation is issued, the clock starts ticking — but in many parts of the city, it never seems to stop. The map below shows the average number of days a violation remains open before any resolution. Across the board, lower-income zip codes see violations linger significantly longer than wealthier areas, even for the most dangerous Class C cases that pose an immediate risk to residents.

This gap reveals a second layer of inequity: it is not only that poor neighbourhoods receive more violations, but that those violations are left to fester for longer. The burden of an unresolved hazard falls entirely on the tenant while the system stalls.
