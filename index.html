01 The Scale of the Problem

Every year, hundreds of thousands of New Yorkers file a housing complaint. Most never see real change.

Between 2021 and 2025, New York City's Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) received over half a million complaints about housing conditions — broken heating, mold, pest infestations, structural damage. Each one is a family living in an unsafe home.

Yet the data shows a stark divide. The volume of violations and complaints is not spread evenly across the city. The Bronx, year after year, leads every other borough in complaint volume. Brooklyn follows. Meanwhile, Manhattan and Staten Island tell a very different story.

02 What Are People Complaining About?

The complaints reveal a housing system under chronic stress.

Not all housing violations are the same. A broken elevator is different from a gas leak. Missing heat in January is different from a cracked wall. The data breaks complaints into categories — and the type of problem varies significantly by borough.

Across all boroughs, heating and plumbing failures are the most common. But in the Bronx, pests and structural violations appear at higher rates than anywhere else — signs of buildings that have been chronically undermaintained, not just temporarily broken.

03 How Long Does It Take?

Filing a complaint is only the beginning. Waiting for a fix is where the system breaks down.

Even when a violation is officially recorded, it doesn't mean the problem gets fixed quickly. The dataset tracks how many days it takes from when a violation is issued to when it is officially closed. The results are striking.

In the Bronx, the average violation takes over 60 days to close. In Staten Island, the same process takes under 30. That is a gap of more than a month — consistent across all four years in the data.

For a resident without heat in January, a month is not an abstraction. It is a month of cold.

04 — What the data tells us

The Bronx is not unlucky. It is systematically neglected.

Year after year, the same pattern holds. The Bronx has the most complaints, the most violations, and the longest wait times for resolution. This is not random variation — the data points to a structural inequality in how New York City's housing enforcement system operates.

Complaints are not equally likely to become violations. In some boroughs, a high share of complaints lead to formal violations being issued — a sign that inspectors are responding. In others, complaints disappear into a system with too little capacity and too little urgency.

Housing is infrastructure. When it fails, it fails families. The data makes clear that in New York City, the zip code you live in is one of the strongest predictors of whether your housing problem will be addressed — and how quickly.
