The City of Dreams?

Every year, more than 300,000 people move to New York City in pursuit of a better life. Many drawn by opportunity, others by the promise that ambition alone is enough. However, this dream often comes with a steep price.

Data analysis by [ Maria Vendas & Diogo Forte ] · Final project · DTU

A System at the Breaking Point

As New York's housing stock continues to age, the systems designed to protect its residents are fracturing. Buildings go years without basic repairs. Heating fails. Mold spreads. Structural damage is ignored. Every year, around the winter months, the city's Housing Preservation and Development agency (HPD) receives millions of complaints about housing conditions.

The city's Housing Preservation and Development agency (HPD) tracks these crises through two lenses: complaints filed directly whenever a tenant calls 311 to report an issue, and formal violations issued after an inspector visits. Together, these datasets reveal who is left behind, and who is responsible.

Volume of Voice: 3 Million Pleas for Repair

Between January 1 of 2021 and December 31 of 2025, HPD received over 3 million complaints. Rather than analysing all complaint types, we focused on the five categories that most directly signal unsafe or unliveable conditions: Heat/Hot Water, Unsanitary Conditions, Water Leaks, Plumbing, and Electric. These were selected because they are the most relevant to evaluate housing conditions and together account for 2,468,069 of the total recorded cases over the period.

While the volume of complaints has risen city-wide, the map below reveals a stark geographic reality: housing distress is deeply concentrated. By looking at the city by zip code, we can see exactly where the system is under the most pressure.

Fig. 2 — The Geography of Housing Distress

Source: NYC Open Data — HPD Service Requests.

The Bronx
With 595 complaints per 1,000 residents, the Bronx significantly outpaces the rest of the city — reporting 82% more issues than Manhattan (327 per 1,000) and 124% more than Brooklyn (266 per 1,000).
Top Hotspots
Four of the top five zip codes are in the Bronx (10452, 10457, 10458, 10467), with 11226 in Flatbush, Brooklyn.

Raw numbers only tell half the story. While Brooklyn houses the city's largest population (2.6M), its complaint volume is actually outpaced by the Bronx. Despite being the second-least populated borough with only 1.4M residents, the Bronx demarks itself as the epicenter of housing distress.

When normalized by population, the density of pleas for repair in the Bronx creates a stark outlier on the map. These are not just statistics, they represent a concentrated failure of infrastructure in neighborhoods home to the city's most vulnerable low-income families.

Breaking this distress down by category reveals the nature of the crisis. From heating failures to plumbing emergencies, use the toggles below to see how different forms of neglect shift across the boroughs and over time.

Heat & Hot Water
By far the most severe crisis, peaking in 2025 with nearly 80 complaints per 1,000 residents in the Bronx alone. This category represents over 52% of all citywide filings.
The Bronx Outlier
In every single category, from Electrical to Unsanitary Conditions, the Bronx maintains a massive lead, often reporting double or triple the complaint density of Queens or Staten Island.

Taken together, the data paints a clear and troubling picture of systemic neglect. Heat and Hot Water failures dominate the narrative, pointing to structural rot in aging heating infrastructure that spikes aggressively during the winter months.

Across four of the five categories, Electric, Plumbing, Heat, and Unsanitary Conditions, we see an alarming upward trend as we approach 2025, suggesting that housing conditions are deteriorating rather than improving.

The geographic disparity remains the most striking finding: while Staten Island and Queens maintain relatively low, stable complaint densities, the Bronx exists in a different reality entirely, with complaint rates that continue to climb higher than any other borough in the city.

From Words to Violations: A Surge in Confirmed Neglect

A complaint is an SOS, a violation is the city's official confirmation that a home is legally unfit. Between 2021 and 2025, the volume of these confirmed hazards grew relentlessly, peaking at over 900,000 total violations citywide in a single year.

This surge suggests that inspectors are finding more than just the initial issue reported by tenants. Often, a single visit for a leak reveals a cascade of secondary issues, leading to the staggering 3.9 million violations issued over this five-year period.

Fig. 4 — Stacked Violations by Borough per Year

Source: NYC Open Data — HPD Violations. Dashed line indicates a 400,000 violation threshold.

2024 Peak
Violations reached an all-time high in 2024, with the Bronx and Brooklyn combined accounting for more than half of the city's total documented housing failures.
The 400k Threshold
By 2024, the volume of violations in the Bronx alone approached the 400,000 mark — a volume nearly double its 2021 levels.

The stacked data reveals a troubling reality: while every borough saw an increase in violations through 2024, the burden is not shared equally. The Bronx and Brooklyn consistently form the foundation of this chart, representing the vast majority of the city's housing distress.

But volume is only one metric; severity is another. To understand the stakes for a tenant, we must look at how the city classifies these failures. HPD utilizes a three-tier hierarchy of danger:

  • Class A (Non-Hazardous): Minor quality-of-life issues like peeling paint or dripping faucets.
  • Class B (Hazardous): Serious conditions such as mold, pests, or a lack of carbon monoxide detectors.
  • Class C (Immediately Hazardous): Life-threatening emergencies, including a lack of heat, hot water, or structural integrity.
Fig. 5 — Violation Density by Severity Class (2021–2025)

Source: NYC Open Data. Normalized per 1,000 residents.

The Bronx Surge
While other boroughs remain relatively flat, the Bronx shows an aggressive upward trajectory, peaking in 2024 with over 100 Class B violations per 1,000 residents.
Class A Parity
Class A is the only category where the Bronx and Brooklyn share the lead. In 2021, Brooklyn actually surpassed the Bronx before trends diverged in later years.
The Severity Gap
In the most dangerous category (Class C), the Bronx reports more than double the violation density of Manhattan and Brooklyn by 2024.

Analyzing violations by class exposes the widening gap in housing safety. For Class A issues, the city appears to follow a similar downward or stable trend. However, as we move into Class B (Hazardous) and Class C (Immediately Hazardous), the Bronx breaks away from the citywide baseline.

By 2024, the density of life-threatening (Class C) violations in the Bronx reached 83 per 1,000 residents, a figure that dwarfs the 32 per 1,000 seen in Manhattan. This suggests that the crisis in the Bronx isn't just about more issues, it is about significantly more dangerous issues.

Across all charts, Staten Island consistently registers the lowest violation density, rarely exceeding 10 violations per 1,000 residents even in the most severe categories.

Is the Income Gap a Housing Gap?

The geographic concentration of housing distress is not a coincidence of architecture, but a reflection of economic power. By overlaying HPD data with median household income, a striking pattern emerges: crisis zones of the city map almost perfectly onto the neighborhoods with the least financial resilience .

The correlation between income and housing distress is not simply strong: it is consistent across every category, every year, and every severity class examined in this analysis. Where median household income falls below the city average, complaint and violation rates rise. This is not a simple statistical artefact, but the material reality that low-income renters have the least ability to demand repairs.

The Manhattan contrast makes the inequality legible at a glance. Just a few miles from the highest-distress zip codes in the Bronx, neighbourhoods in Manhattan register near-zero complaint and violation densities.

A Clock That Never Stops

Complaints and violations are the city's official ledger of distress, but for a tenant, the only metric that truly matters is time. Once a violation is issued, the clock starts ticking toward a legal deadline for repair, yet many cases remain open for months or even years, leaving tenants in limbo with unresolved hazards.

While we have established that low-income areas suffer from the highest density of hazards—peaking at over 350 violations per 1,000 residents—the question remains: does the city's ability to resolve these issues also track with the wealth of the zip code?

The "Days Open" map (Left) reveals a system that does not always follow a simple, linear income gradient. While the most distressed zip codes often face systemic delays, the reality of unresolved violations is citywide.

Some low-income pockets in the Bronx record mid-range waiting times, while certain higher-income areas in Manhattan also show prolonged open periods. Across the city, violations routinely remain unresolved for hundreds of days. The burden of living with an officially recorded hazard while the system stalls falls entirely on the tenant, regardless of borough.

However, when we look specifically at successful resolutions (Right Map), the income disparity becomes starkly legible. The slowest certification times—the darkest reds on the map, exceeding 30 days—are concentrated in the Northern Bronx and Upper Manhattan. These areas align almost perfectly with the low-income categories on our center map.

The average certification gap between the Northern Bronx and Lower Manhattan, despite sharing the same legal framework.

This data suggests a two-tiered system of justice. Hotspots with the highest violation density (350+ per 1,000 residents) are frequently the same areas where paperwork takes the longest to process. For tenants in the poorest neighborhoods, the wait isn't just for a physical repair, it is for the official recognition that their home is once again safe to inhabit.

Behind every violation is a building. Behind every building is an owner.

While geographic patterns reveal where the crisis is most acute, the ownership data reveals the actors most directly responsible. A small group of landlords accounts for a disproportionate share of the city’s documented neglect. For these "worst offenders," violations are not isolated incidents but a systemic byproduct of their business model.

Fig. 9 — The Worst Offenders

Source: NYC Open Data — HPD Registrations. Owners identified by shared registration IDs across multiple buildings. Portfolio breakdown reflects zip code income classification for each property.

The data above brings the concentration of negligence into sharp focus. Leading the list is Zohar Levy, whose portfolio of 15 buildings consists entirely of low-income housing. This pattern repeats down the list: for the top owners like Alex Arker and Allan Arker (9 buildings each), there is a complete absence of properties in high-income areas. In fact, out of the dozens of landlords listed, only one—Nan Nan—holds a single building in a high-income zip code.

The table exposes a grim economic reality: these portfolios are not scattered randomly across the city; they are meticulously concentrated in the same underserved neighborhoods that dominate every map in this report. Many of these entities, such as Arverne Preservation LLC or HP Savoy Park II HDFC, operate through corporate structures that can obscure direct accountability while their buildings accumulate violations year after year.

This is not merely incidental neglect. By focusing their holdings almost exclusively in "Low" and "Low-Mid" income zones, these landlords operate a model that depends on the extraction of rent from tenants with the fewest options and the least leverage to escalate repairs. In this context, the thousands of open violations are not administrative accidents—they are the predictable output of a deliberate strategy that prioritizes minimal maintenance over human safety.

Findings

A city of unequal shelter

The data assembled in this report presents a coherent and troubling picture of New York City's housing crisis. Complaints, violations, and resolution times all converge on the same geographic and economic fault lines: the lowest-income neighbourhoods bear the greatest burden, face the most hazardous conditions, and wait the longest for relief.

The Bronx stands as the clearest example of systemic neglect — consistently leading across every metric, from complaints per resident to Class C violation density to unresolved days. Flatbush in Brooklyn follows closely. Meanwhile, a small group of property owners account for a disproportionate share of the problem, underscoring that concentrated landlord accountability could be a lever for meaningful change.

In a city where housing is both scarce and expensive, the evidence shows that the burden of unsafe, degraded, and unresponsive housing falls almost entirely on those least able to absorb it. The skyline promises opportunity. The data reveals a system that continues to leave the most vulnerable left behind.
